M²AdaptVLA: Multimodal Memory-Augmented Vision-Language-Action Model with Predictive Test-Time Adaptation

Abstract

Effective robotic manipulation calls for multimodal memory and rapid adaptation to shifts in contact dynamics. However, many existing policies, which retain either visual or wrench history, may miss key cues from the other modality. Moreover, even with wrench memory, fixed policies can struggle when contact conditions shift at deployment. To this end, we introduce M²AdaptVLA, a vision-language-action model that integrates visual and wrench memories with predictive test-time adaptation. For visual memory, the model uses test-time training (TTT) to encode observation history in fast weights. Each entry in the bounded TTT bank combines the corresponding frame's features with a readout from these weights, preserving cues that predate the oldest retained frame. For wrench memory, a context gate controls how much the action expert draws on wrench history as it jointly denoises future actions and wrenches. For predictive test-time adaptation, the auxiliary wrench prediction loss also provides online self-supervision. Using measured wrenches as targets, we update low-rank policy adapters by backpropagating only through the final denoising step. Real-world experiments show that the TTT bank outperforms visual memory baselines on progress tracking, scene recall, and demonstration following. On contact-sensitive tasks, the context gate yields higher success rates than direct injection or gated cross-attention. Predictive test-time adaptation improves robustness to shifts in contact dynamics. On eight tasks requiring visual and wrench memory, M²AdaptVLA achieves a mean success rate of 82.71%, a 31.46% absolute gain over the strongest baseline.

Introduction

Method

Experiment

We evaluated M²AdaptVLA on two bimanual robots to answer four questions.

  1. (Q1) How does M²AdaptVLA compare with state-of-the-art VLA baselines on tasks that rely on visual and wrench memory?
  2. (Q2) What advantages does the TTT bank offer over existing visual memory methods?
  3. (Q3) Does the context gate outperform existing methods for injecting wrench history into the policy?
  4. (Q4) Can a policy with predictive test-time adaptation better handle shifts in contact dynamics at deployment than a fixed policy?

Experimental setup

The TeleTwin Desktop and Tianji each use a front camera, two wrist cameras, and six-axis wrist wrench sensors. The eight tasks probe visual memory, wrench memory, or both. The dataset contains 5,953 demonstrations. We trained the policy on eight NVIDIA A800 GPUs and ran inference and online adaptation on a single RTX 5090.

For whole-task success, the robot had to complete every required stage. We evaluated each policy over 30 trials on Open Safe and 20 on each of the other seven tasks. In the Open Safe ablations, we scored each of the nine stages separately over 60 trials per variant.

Q1. M²AdaptVLA outperforms the VLA baselines

Across eight tasks, M²AdaptVLA achieved 82.71% mean success, 31.46 percentage points above the strongest baseline, π-Mem (51.25%). It ranked first on seven tasks and tied for first on Unplug.

The baselines’ strengths varied with the task. On Button Sequence, both visual memory baselines achieved 55% success and both force-aware baselines scored 0%. On USB-C Insertion and Removal, the force-aware baselines reached 40–50% and the visual memory baselines reached 15–30%. M²AdaptVLA achieved 80% on Button Sequence and 65% on USB-C Insertion and Removal.

Q2. The TTT bank improves progress tracking, initial-scene recall, and demonstration following

On the same WALL-X backbone, the TTT bank completed 69.7% of individual keypresses during safe passcode entry. GDN achieved 35.0%, Frame Bank 34.0%, TTT only 30.0%, and RoboTTT-style 12.7%.

Updating fast weights after a fixed number of action steps provides an implicit progress cue. Each bank entry also combines the frame’s visual features with the history available at that decision. Later entries can therefore retain an early cue, such as which drawer was initially open, after that frame leaves the bank.

For initial-scene recall, the TTT bank reopened the correct drawer in 90% of trials, compared with 75% for TTT only, the strongest alternative. For demonstration following, it reproduced the button order in 80% of trials, compared with 60% for RoboTTT-style.

Q3. The context gate outperforms direct wrench injection and gated cross-attention

With the same TTT bank, the context gate achieved 79.6% mean stage success on Open Safe. Direct injection into the action expert reached 69.4%, direct injection into the VLM reached 31.5%, and gated cross-attention (GCA) reached 48.5%. Compared with direct action-expert input, the context gate increased key insertion success from 51.7% to 75.0% and final keypad press success from 38.3% to 70.0%.

In the same Open Safe episode, the context gate gave wrench history greater weight during key insertion, when resistance can indicate misalignment, and less weight as the unlocked door swung open. The GCA gate, computed from pooled wrench features, remained nearly constant in this episode.

Adding future wrench prediction as auxiliary supervision raised keypad press success from 71.0% to 75.7% and mean stage success from 79.6% to 83.3%.

Q4. Predictive test-time adaptation improves robustness to shifts in contact dynamics

Compared with the fixed policy, updating the first four layers raised wiping success from 90% to 100% when the whole board was raised, and from 80% to 95% when the board was flipped and its rear edge raised. Both policies observed wrist wrenches. The adaptive policy also updated its adapters using the error between predicted and measured wrenches.

Across the two conditions, mean success was 97.5% with four-layer adaptation, 87.5% with all 24 layers, and 85.0% with the fixed policy. Updating four layers took 159.5 ms per update on the RTX 5090, compared with 267.7 ms for all layers.

Plug insertion with a raised power strip

We also evaluated adaptation when a board eraser raised one side of the power strip, changing the height and orientation of its sockets. Each configuration was evaluated over 20 trials; success required fully inserting and releasing the plug.

Updating layers 1–4 improved success from 75% to 85%, a gain of 10 percentage points (13.3% relative), while updating all 24 layers achieved 70%. Consistent with the wiping results, adapting more layers did not necessarily improve robustness to changed contact conditions.

Conclusion

In this work, we introduced M²AdaptVLA, a vision-language-action policy that integrates visual and wrench memories with predictive test-time adaptation. In real-robot experiments, its TTT bank outperformed visual memory baselines in tracking task progress, recalling the initial scene, and following demonstrated sequences. The context gate adjusted the influence of wrench history across task stages. Predictive test-time adaptation improved robustness to changes in contact conditions with low update latency. Across eight real-robot tasks, M²AdaptVLA achieved 82.71% mean success, an absolute gain of 31.46% over the strongest baseline. A limitation of our approach is that maintaining both visual and wrench memories increases overall inference latency. In future work, we will explore a two-timescale architecture in which a slow module updates multimodal memory and a fast controller uses the latest memory and current observations to select actions in real time.
